Part A

On your own

About 55 minutes. Don't rush. Your thinking now is what you'll bring to your group.

A1

First take

5 min
A1.1

Sagan's title starts from the idea that Iran must be kept from the bomb. Did you agree with that going in? Did the article change your mind or strengthen it?

A1.2

What's one thing in the article that confused you, surprised you, or annoyed you?

You'll come back to this with your group.

A2

Map the argument

15 min

Before you can agree or disagree with Sagan, make sure you know what he actually says. Use the text, not memory.

A2.1

Write Sagan's thesis in one sentence, in your own words.

A2.2

The article has seven sections. What is the main point of each? Keep it short.

A2.3 p. 2

Sagan says governments usually pursue nuclear weapons for one of three reasons. What are they? Which one does he think best explains Iran, and why?

A2.4 pp. 2-4

Sagan points to countries that gave up or curbed their nuclear ambitions, "the nuclear dogs that did not bark." Find at least three. What convinced each one to stop?

A3

Dig into the guiding questions

20 min

These are the questions from your reading guide. For each one, find at least one line in the text that supports your answer and note the page.

A3.1

What does Sagan mean by "deterrence optimism" and "proliferation fatalism"? Why does he reject both?

Put each idea in your own words. Then explain how the two "reinforce each other in a disturbing way" (p. 1).

A3.2

How does Sagan challenge the idea that nuclear weapons kept the peace during the Cold War?

He calls it dangerous learning by "trial and terror." Which of his examples worries you most, and why?

A3.3

Why does Sagan argue that a nuclear Iran would be more like Pakistan than the Soviet Union or China?

What went wrong in Pakistan after 1998? Who is the IRGC, and why does Sagan think it matters who guards Iran's nuclear sites?

A3.4

What role do institutions, norms, and agreements (like the NPT) play in limiting nuclear weapons?

Explain the "I won't if you won't" idea (p. 4) in your own words. How does that reflect a more liberal approach to international relations?

A3.5

What solutions does Sagan propose to prevent Iran from obtaining nuclear weapons?

Why does he reject military strikes? Why is Libya a bad model and North Korea's 1994 deal a better one? What would each side have to give up?

★ BIG QUESTION

According to Sagan, does allowing more countries to obtain nuclear weapons make the world safer or more dangerous? Why?

First answer as Sagan would. Then answer for yourself.

A4

Test the argument

10 min

Understanding Sagan doesn't mean agreeing with him. Pick at least two of these.

A4.1

How strong is the Pakistan comparison? What evidence does Sagan use to say Iran would behave the same way, and what's missing?

A4.2

Sagan's best model, the 1994 Agreed Framework, broke down, and North Korea got the bomb anyway. Does that weaken his case or support it?

A4.3

Sagan says the United States must give up the threat of regime change by force. Is that realistic? Is the trade he proposes fair to both sides?

A4.4

Who is Sagan writing for, and whose point of view is at the centre? Who decides which states are "responsible" and which are "rogue"?

A5

Since 2006

5 min

Sagan wrote this nearly 20 years ago. A lot has happened with Iran's nuclear program since.

A5.1

What do you know (or can you quickly find) about what's happened with Iran's nuclear program since 2006? Has anything like Sagan's plan been tried? Do those events make Sagan look right, wrong, or neither?

Part B

With your group

About 20 minutes. Bring your answers from Part A. Everyone shares before anyone debates.

B

B2

B3

B4
